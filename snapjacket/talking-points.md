## What it is

SnapJacket is a **patent-pending clamshell-design PVC piling repair jacket** that wraps around a damaged wood, concrete, or composite piling and is filled with concrete or grout in the annular space between the host pile and the jacket. The result is a watertight, structural, UV-resistant encasement that restores full structural integrity to the original piling — without removing the deck above, without bringing in cranes or barges, and using only basic hand tools any contractor already owns.

The patent-pending **sliding lock** on the jacket seam ensures a tight, watertight seal, blocking sun, oxygen, and the marine organisms (gribbles, teredo / shipworm, fungi) that destroy the piling in the first place. The DuroSleeve™ logo on the SnapJacket submittal document refers to the underlying PVC extrusion technology — for sales conversations, treat "SnapJacket" as the customer-facing product name.

The brochure positions it directly: *"the best solution available to repair existing pilings that have been damaged by marine organisms, the ravages of time, elements and harsh marine environments."*

## The problem it solves

Wood pilings rot. Modern wood pilings rot faster than they used to — modern timber is grown faster, less dense, and (because EPA pressure has reduced allowable CCA treatment levels in many states) is *less* chemically protected than the wood pilings installed 30 years ago. The result is that an older dock built on 30-year wood is reaching end-of-life *and the homeowner cannot just replace the wood with the same wood and expect another 30 years.* The replacement wood, even when treated, often fails in 15 years or less.

Replacing a piling means demolishing the deck above it. That's a five-figure job for a residential dock. SnapJacket fixes the piling in place — the deck stays, the boat stays, and the contractor is in and out in a fraction of the time.

It also stops further chemical leaching from the encased CCA-treated wood into the water — the jacket seals the toxic substrate inside, eliminating the ongoing environmental damage.

## Materials and engineering specs

The PVC compound is extruded uniaxially-oriented PVC-M (PVC-Modified) with the following properties from the SnapJacket Submittal & Application document:

- **Compound certification:** NSF Standard 61 (potable water rated) — meaning it's safe enough for drinking-water systems, with zero lead. Powerful trust-builder line for any environmental-objection conversation.
- **Cell classification:** 12111 per ASTM D-1784
- **Hardness:** 80 Shore D
- **Tensile strength at break:** 150–300 (ASTM D-638)
- **Tensile modulus:** 2,206 MPa
- **Flexural modulus:** 2,413 MPa
- **Impact strength:** 20 kJ/m² Izod at 20°C; 8 kJ/m² Izod at 0°C (ASTM D-256)
- **Abrasion resistance:** 260,000 cycles with minimal wear (gravel + river sand)
- **Fire performance:** Oxygen index 0.45, flame spread index 0 — *does not support combustion*
- **UV weathering:** Maintains the same level of UV protection as vinyl siding and vinyl windows, with an exposure rating in excess of **50 years**
- **Microbiological resistance:** Immune to microbiological and macrobiological attack (testing conducted at Technical University of Darmstadt, Germany)

## Available sizes

- **Diameters:** 10", 12", 14", 16" (jacket inside diameters)
- **Lengths:** 7.5 feet standard stock; custom lengths available on request
- **Color:** Gray standard; alternative colors on request
- **Annular space:** designed for a 2" minimum annulus between host pile and jacket for structural applications. Tighter 1" fits possible with appropriate concrete/grout selection (Sand Topping Mix flows into tight spaces and sets to ~4,000 psi).

### Sizing rule of thumb (for the rep's cheat-sheet)

Austin sizes by **circumference of the host pile**, not diameter:

- 36" circumference → 12" jacket
- 29" circumference → 10" jacket
- ~37–38" circumference → standard 12" jacket fit

If the contractor is between sizes or the host pile has irregular profile (concrete bell, marine growth, prior repair), **go up a size**. A 16" jacket on a pile that *technically* fits a 14" is far easier to fill cleanly with concrete than a 14" jacket fighting tight tolerances. Material cost differential is negligible vs. the labor of fighting a tight install.

## Install workflow (one-paragraph version for the deck)

Clean the host pile of marine growth with a powerwasher. Cut the SnapJacket to the required length with a fine-tooth Sawzall or Dremel circular saw. Excavate the soil around the pile bottom enough that the jacket can extend 12" below the mudline and have room to snap together. Open the bottom section, place around the top of the piling, slide it down. Snap the lock together (a small ratchet strap helps if needed). Position so the jacket extends 12" below mudline and 12" above the mean high water line. Pump out water from the annular space. Fill the annulus with ready-mix concrete (≥1.5" space) or Type M mortar mix (<1.5" space). For jackets >12" diameter and >10' long, support the jacket with a ratchet strap during fill. After the concrete sets, apply a grout crown at the top, sloped away from the pile to prevent water pooling.

## Common customer questions (from email vault)

**Q: How do I remove the existing concrete bell or black-pipe form before installing SnapJacket?**
A: Pneumatic chisels break concrete easily underwater. Cutting through the plastic shell of the old form is harder — toothed blades work but require caution underwater. A standard cut-off disk will melt plastic on land, but underwater the water cools the plastic faster than the disk can melt it, so cut-off disks are ineffective. Often the cleanest answer is to leave the old form in place and install a SnapJacket large enough to wrap around it.

**Q: My pile has dry rot above the waterline. Will the SnapJacket protect that part?**
A: Yes, but apply a layer of stretch wrap to the dry portion of the pile *above the waterline* before installing the jacket. Reason: moisture from the freshly placed concrete can wick up into the dry wood, the wood swells, and the swelling can blow out the jacket-and-concrete encasement. The stretch wrap is a moisture barrier that prevents this. **This is gold for sales credibility — it's the kind of detail only someone who has installed thousands of jackets would know.**

**Q: How do I size the jacket — by diameter or circumference?**
A: Always by circumference. Wrap a measuring tape around the host pile at the worst-rotted point, add 2" for annular space, add another 1–2" if you want extra room for clean concrete fill, and pick the next-larger jacket diameter that fits.

**Q: Can I use it for piles bigger than 16"?**
A: Custom lengths and oversized jackets are available on request. Lead time longer.

## Common objections (and how to handle them)

**"Won't I crack the pole or the jacket if I tighten the through-bolts too hard?"**
A: No. You cannot snap a SnapJacket header board in half by over-torquing a nut and washer before you visibly deflect the pole — and contractors are typically *too timid* with these connections, not too aggressive. Torque them down like a timber pile. The right zone: when the nut starts collapsing into the wood and treatment squeezes out, you're done. Use double nuts or nylon lock nuts to prevent storm back-off.

**"What happens when a jacket cracks or fails?"**
A: It's rare, but Shoreline owns the problem. When Eastern Marine in Pennsylvania had two older jackets bust during a storm, Austin treated it as a service call — ordered replacement units, his team pulled them and shipped them out, problem solved. Lesson learned from that case (worth keeping in your back pocket): **don't over-fill the pole with concrete.** Filling concrete higher than the header-board line creates dead weight at the top of the pile, which adds swaying momentum during big storms. Some flex is good. Skyscrapers flex in wind for the same reason — overly rigid structures tear themselves apart.

**"It's just plastic. How is plastic going to last 25 years in saltwater?"**
A: The PVC compound is the same chemistry used in vinyl siding and vinyl windows, with a documented UV exposure rating in excess of 50 years. It's NSF-rated for potable water — meaning it's safe enough for drinking-water systems. Marine organisms can't eat PVC; macroborganisms (teredo, gribbles) physically can't digest it. Microorganisms can't break it down. The 25-year warranty isn't aspirational — it's conservative based on the documented UV and biological resistance numbers.

## Failure modes / what we've learned (don't hide these — own them)

- **Older jackets can fail in heavy storms** — primarily from over-rigid concrete fill creating top-heavy dead weight. Modern install practice: fill only to the header-board level, not the full pile. This was the Eastern Marine lesson.
- **Tight 1" annulus tolerance** — works only with the right concrete (Sand Topping Mix). Standard ready-mix concrete won't flow into 1" gaps cleanly. When in doubt, upsize the jacket and use standard concrete.
- **Marine growth on old forms** dulls cutting tools fast. Plan tool replacement / extra blades into the labor budget.
- **Submittal note for engineered jobs:** "If you are using the Snap Jacket to repair a supporting structure on decayed pilings then structural engineering should be obtained." Use this language when a Master Halco contractor pushes for SnapJacket on a heavily-loaded commercial application — the structural engineer review is part of due diligence, not a hedge.
