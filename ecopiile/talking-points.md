## What it is

EcoPile is Shoreline's **vinyl-fiberglass composite structural piling** — a semi-hollow extruded pole engineered as a true drop-in replacement for traditional wood pilings in residential and light-commercial marine construction. It uses the same standard tools, the same pile drivers, the same dimensions, and the same connection hardware as a wood pile. From the contractor's standpoint, the install workflow is identical to wood. From the property owner's standpoint, it's the foundation of the dock that *finally* lasts as long as the deck above it.

The pitch deck describes EcoPile as **"semi-hollow PVC + chopped-glass piling with honeycomb webs."** The brochure calls the internal structure a **"hexagram internal truss system"** — six-pointed-star webbing inside the pole that gives it structural rigidity far beyond a hollow pipe. Both names refer to the same engineering: the cross-section looks like a Star of David inside a circular outer wall.

EcoPile has been in the field for **8+ years with exceptional feedback and repeat customers**, and projects built on it have won multiple national building awards.

## The problem it solves

There are two compounding problems EcoPile addresses, and the deck should hit both:

### 1. Wood pilings used to last 30+ years. Now they're failing in less than half that.

The brochure is direct about this: *"Homeowners used to see 20 to 30+ years of life out of a wooden piling, but now it is not uncommon for a wood piling to fail in less than half that time."* Two reasons: modern timber is grown faster (less dense, weaker), and EPA pressure has reduced the allowable concentration of CCA preservative treatment. The wood is *less protected* and *less durable* than the wood pilings homeowners are used to seeing last decades.

### 2. The piling is the foundation of the dock — and replacing pilings means demolishing everything above them.

Quote directly from the brochure on this — it's the strongest single argument in the entire EcoPile sales playbook:

> *"The pilings are the foundation of the dock, and that is the first place you should look when choosing composites. You can easily replace a deck 10 years down the road, but if your pilings fail, the whole structure will need to be replaced. Switching all of your pilings to the EcoPile will result in a small increase in overall job costs upfront, but you will never have to worry about your foundation again."*

A homeowner installing composite decking is already accepting that they want a dock that lasts. Putting composite decking on top of wood pilings is **building a Ferrari on a foundation made of cardboard.** That framing closes residential customers reliably.

## Materials and engineering specs

- **Composition:** Rigid PVC matrix with **chopped fiberglass** mixed in during the extrusion process. The chopped glass adds tensile and impact resistance; the PVC keeps cost down. The outer skin is **UV/IR-resistant** and adds impact resistance to the surface.
- **Internal structure:** **Hexagram (six-point star) internal truss webbing.** The triangulated cross-section dramatically increases stiffness over a hollow pipe.
- **Bottom retention:** **Spiral grooved retention rings** cut into the bottom section that's driven into the ground. Sand and substrate grip the grooves; this combined with the webbing surface area resists settling far better than a smooth wood pile.
- **Recycled content:** Up to **10 lbs of recycled PVC reused per linear foot of piling.** A 30' piling = ~300 lbs of plastic kept out of landfill / waterway. Per the brochure: *"For every single EcoPile you choose to use, you are saving several trees over the lifespan of the piling."*
- **End-of-life:** 100% recyclable.
- **Leaching:** Zero. *"100% free from any leaching pollutants, 100% recyclable, 100% impervious to wood boring organisms."*

### Available sizes

- **Lengths:** 16', 20', 31', 36' standard; custom lengths available

### Recent formulation upgrades (cite these — they shut down the "EcoPile is too flexible" objection)

- **~6 months prior to today:** New proprietary PVC-fiberglass formulation. Result: **~25% increase in rigidity**, better fiberglass-to-PVC fusion, improved impact resistance, more consistent extrusion.
- **~3 months prior:** New 12" wall thickness specification — wall **20–25% thicker** than the prior 12" generation. Combined with the new formulation, the current 12" EcoPile is **~40% stiffer than the prior 12" generation.**
- **Practical effect:** The new 12" significantly reduces or eliminates the need for concrete fill in many freestanding-lift and rough-water applications that previously required it. Less concrete = less labor, faster install, and (counterintuitively) better storm survival.

This is from Austin's email correspondence with Barry Bishop at Trident Marine Construction (Tampa) — describing the aftermath of **Hurricane Sally** in the Pensacola / Gulf Coast area. While EcoPile-built docks survived intact, **neighboring properties built on concrete pilings were "completely wiped out."** Marina owners watched it happen and started converting their floating-structure foundations to EcoPile.

The mechanism — and the counter-intuitive insight worth landing on the slide — is this: **rigid structures tear themselves apart in hurricanes.** Wave loading is not a static load. It's cyclic. A pile that flexes a small, controlled amount absorbs energy and survives. A pile that's perfectly rigid (concrete, or an over-filled-with-concrete EcoPile) transmits the full energy into the joints and the structure above. Skyscrapers flex in wind for the same engineering reason.

Austin knows of *"a dozen commercial projects now that have taken no damage from several storms."* Insurance companies in post-Helene rebuild zones are increasingly nervous about anything other than composite-piling foundations.

## How EcoPile beats the alternatives

### vs. Wood pilings (with or without protective wraps)

- Wood rots. Wraps cost $50–$125 per pole and **don't stop rot — they slow it.** Eventually they fail.
- Wood lifespan is collapsing — see "modern timber is grown faster" above. New wood pilings can fail in 15 years in marine service.
- EcoPile lasts a lifetime. Total cost of ownership over a 30-year horizon strongly favors EcoPile despite higher upfront cost.

### vs. Solid fiberglass / "FRP" pilings (Pearson FRP and similar)

- Austin's clarification on terminology — **memorize this for technical conversations**: *"FRP is a pretty loose term. Most people use it wrong. They call a pure fiberglass object an 'FRP piling,' but FRP really means 'fiber reinforced polymer,' which technically fits our products [fiber-reinforced PVC] much better than pure fiberglass."* So when a contractor asks for "an FRP piling," they often mean Pearson-style solid fiberglass — but EcoPile is itself a fiber-reinforced polymer.
- Solid fiberglass is great structurally. It is also **roughly twice the price.** *"At about half the price of comparable 100% fiberglass pilings, choosing the EcoPile should be a no-brainer."*
- The brochure framing: *"This allows us to utilize the strength of fiberglass while keeping the production cost down."*

### vs. Concrete pilings

- Concrete shears under cyclic lateral storm loading. **Hurricane Sally case study.**
- Concrete pilings require specialty equipment to install (cranes, drivers, forming). EcoPile uses standard wood-piling rigs.
- Concrete leaches alkalinity and contributes to local water-chemistry shifts in saltwater applications.
- Concrete cracks. Once it cracks, rebar corrodes. Once rebar corrodes, structural failure is on a timer.

## Common customer questions (with answers from the email vault)

### "Doesn't EcoPile flex too much, especially in deep water or rough conditions?"

This is the #1 objection in the email vault, and Austin's full response is the most comprehensive objection-handling sequence in the entire archive. Internalize it:

- *"Like any new product, EcoPile has gone through a few learning curves."* Open with humility.
- The original product was designed as a drop-in replacement for timber — *"there are still important differences to keep in mind, much like composite decking often requires shorter spans than traditional wood."* The composite-decking analogy is gold for residential customers who already accept that composite materials behave a little differently.
- Free-standing pile comparisons (lab demonstrations, contractor poke-test) show "give." But once integrated into a full dock structure (bolted, cross-braced, deck above), the system becomes very stable. *"The composite decking analogy applies."*
- A real-world example Austin uses: *"I once saw an article online critiquing 8" EcoPiles on a long walkway. But the background of the photo showed every single neighboring wood dock was cross-braced too. If the location is rough enough that wood needs bracing, EcoPile needs bracing. The conclusion that the EcoPile was the problem was wrong — it was the location."*
- The recent formulation upgrades (25% stiffer formulation, 40% stiffer 12" wall) have reduced or eliminated the flex concern in most applications.
- Most importantly: the Hurricane Sally / Pensacola data. *"Some flex is good. Concrete pilings nearby got completely wiped out; ours didn't."*

### "Why does 10" EcoPile cost more than 8" wood?"

- **Apples-to-apples cost includes wraps.** A wood pile alone is cheap. A wood pile with the protective wrap a careful contractor adds runs $50–$125 in additional cost — and the wood still rots eventually.
- **EcoPile is rarely the dominant project cost.** Labor, equipment, decking, hardware, dredging — all bigger line items. Project cost increase from going wood-to-EcoPile is typically **10–20% on residential, 5% on large commercial.**
- *"That's a reasonable investment for a foundation that can last a lifetime."*

### "Can I use EcoPile for a boat lift, especially a side-mounted lift?"

- **Best:** lift mounted inline with the pile (top-mount), with the lift roof transferring load directly down through the pile.
- **Acceptable with care:** side-mounted lifts. Use **oversized curved washers** or **custom brackets** to spread the bolt load across the surface of the pile.
- **The risk to manage:** when a through-bolt is tightened, it lands somewhere on the rotational orientation of the hexagram cross-section. If it lands on a triangle apex, load transfers cleanly. If it lands in the "moon" between two triangles (the hollow concave area), point-loading over time will pull the material in.
- During pile driving, you can't reliably control which way the hexagram lands. So **don't rely on bolt position — spread the load.** Curved washers, whaler boards, or — for problem installations — Shoreline can fabricate custom machined brackets in-house. Photos exist in the email vault if a contractor asks.

### "Can I splice an EcoPile if I drive it short?"

- Honest answer first: usually it's simpler and faster to drive a new pile than to splice. **Splicing is an exception, not a routine practice.**
- If splicing is necessary, three options:
  1. **PVC coupling (like water pipe)** — cement-joined sleeve. Fastest, but the internal webs don't connect across the splice, so this is a poor choice for high-lateral-load applications. Acceptable for below-mudline splices where the soil absorbs lateral loading.
  2. **Concrete-fill splice** — mate the cut surfaces flat, drive rebar through the center hexagon and through the outer "moons," fill the splice and adjacent area with concrete, finish the seam with marine adhesive and paint for cosmetic appearance. Labor-intensive but structurally strong.
  3. **Interior wood splice** — a 2x4 or 4x4 inserted into the center cavity, screwed through the walls. Cosmetic only — for very low-load applications.

### "Can I use EcoPile for a seawall?"

- Yes. See `seawall (upcoming)/talking-points.md` for the full treatment. Short version: EcoPile installs as **batter pilings** angling back into dead-men ground anchors, with **whaler boards** running across the wall span to distribute bolt load (preferred over individual point washers). For a premium build, pour a concrete cap with cutouts for the pile tops + rebar embedded into the piles — Austin has called this approach *"damn near invincible."*

## Failure modes / what we've learned (own them on the slide if asked)

- **Bolt nuts backing-off during storms.** Happened on the Hernandez Tampa job. Root cause: under-torqued originally — the contractor was afraid of "cracking" the hollow pole, which is a real fear but unfounded for proper installation depths. **Fix: torque firmly (treat like timber piling — when the nut starts collapsing into wood and treatment squeezes out, you're in the right zone), and use double nuts or nylon lock nuts.**
- **Perceived flexibility in open water.** Real, but addressable. Cross-bracing for rough exposure (same as wood). Boathouse / dock structure provides plenty of stability for typical applications. New 12" formulation has eliminated this in most cases.
- **Concrete fill when not needed.** Tempting to over-fill for "extra strength." **Counterproductive in storms** — top-heavy mass creates swaying momentum that tears connections apart. Rule: fill to header-board level only, not the full pile.
